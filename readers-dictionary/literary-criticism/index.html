Literary Criticism

Literary criticism is the practice of thinking, talking, writing, and asking questions about literature. It can be as informal as discussing a favorite novel with a friend or as detailed as an academic analysis of a classic work.

At its core, literary criticism is about exploring why a story works, how it affects readers, and what makes it memorable. Readers often discuss a book's pacing, characters, themes, worldbuilding, dialogue, and ending to better understand their own experience and share it with others.

The Reader's Dictionary approaches literary criticism from a reader's perspective. Rather than focusing on academic terminology, it introduces the concepts that readers naturally use when recommending books, participating in book clubs, writing reviews, or simply talking about what they enjoyed.

Why This Matters

These aren't just literary terms—they're conversation starters.

After finishing a novel, readers often find themselves saying things like:

"The pacing felt slow."

"The ending surprised me."

"The characters had great chemistry."

"The worldbuilding was incredible."

Understanding these ideas makes it easier to explain why a book worked for you—or why it didn't. Whether you're talking with friends, posting a review, or browsing a bookstore, having the right vocabulary helps you express your reading experience more clearly.

Literary criticism doesn't have to be intimidating. Every time readers explain why a story worked for them—or why it didn't—they're engaging in a form of literary criticism. Learning the language of readers simply makes those conversations easier and more meaningful.

"The pacing dragged."

"The ending stuck the landing."

"The worldbuilding was incredible."

"The dialogue felt natural."

"The villain was compelling."

"The romance felt earned."

"The stakes weren't high enough."

As the Reader's Dictionary grows, each of these topics will become its own definition page, creating a library of concepts that helps readers enjoy books, understand them more deeply, and have richer conversations about what they read.
