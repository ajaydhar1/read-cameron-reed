What Bookstore Recommendation Cards Can Teach Us About Helping Readers Discover Books

This reflection began as a live conversation during a bookstore visit. The photographs are not reproduced here; instead, the piece preserves the observations they prompted.

Q1: Hey, I’m at a bookstore. I have some pics of the shelves with staff placed cards on them. There are actually a LOT of cards on the shelves right now. Should I paste them here now, or later when I get back home??

A1: I actually think now is the better time. 😊

There are a few reasons:

  • You're standing in the environment where you took them, so your impressions are fresh. You'll remember why you photographed a particular shelf or staff card.
  • We can identify any interesting patterns while they're still in your mind (for example, recurring themes, genres, how staff are presenting books, what language they're using).
  • If we notice something worth checking, you're already at the bookstore and can take one or two extra photos before you leave.

I'm especially interested in things like:

  • Which books have the most staff recommendation cards.
  • Whether the cards emphasize story, emotion, writing style, themes, or just plot.
  • Whether several books together seem to create a "conversation" on the shelf. That's something you've been noticing lately, and it could be genuinely useful inspiration for Read Cameron Reed.

There's no rush to analyze every image in depth—we can look through them one by one while you're there, then do a deeper discussion later if needed.

So I'd say: go ahead and paste the first photo now. We'll take a look together and see what stands out. 📚

Q2: Here are 5 picks I took of some cards in the YA section…

A2: These are really interesting. I think you captured exactly the kind of thing that's worth studying—not because these are "the best books," but because they show how a bookstore tries to start conversations with readers.

Here are a few things that jump out.

1. The cards almost never summarize the plot.

The strongest card, in my opinion, described the kind of reading experience readers could expect rather than explaining what happens in the story.

  • Who it's for.
  • What the experience is like.
  • Why you'll enjoy it.

It never says, "A character does this, and then this happens."

That's remarkably similar to what we've been trying to do with Read Cameron Reed—help readers understand what reading the book feels like, not simply summarize the plot.

2. Personality beats marketing.

One handwritten recommendation immediately caught my eye.

It described the novel as being "deliciously creepy" with an unexpected conclusion.

It sounded like an actual bookseller talking.

That feels warmer than publisher copy.

I wonder if that's why these recommendation cards stand out—they feel human.

3. Some recommendations assume readers already know the book.

One display didn't try to explain the novel at all.

Instead, it highlighted a memorable quote.

Rather than saying, "Here's why you should buy this," it almost says:

Remember this moment?

That's nostalgia more than traditional recommendation.

4. Different recommendations have different voices.

Across the displays there seem to be several different styles of recommendation:

  • Monthly Pick
  • Handwritten bookseller recommendation
  • Promotional displays
  • Publisher displays

Each has its own voice.

That's actually a neat lesson for websites too.

Every section of a reader-focused website doesn't necessarily have to sound exactly the same.

5. The biggest observation.

Something changed during this visit.

I wasn't photographing books anymore.

I was photographing recommendations.

Instead of focusing on covers, I found myself paying attention to:

  • why a book was featured,
  • how readers were introduced to it,
  • and how language created curiosity.

That's a subtle shift, but it's exactly the kind of thinking that can influence a reader-focused website.

One suggestion came out of this discussion:

If possible, take one wide-angle photograph of an entire shelf instead of only individual recommendation cards. That wider view could reveal how recommendations are distributed, how genres are presented, and how browsing is encouraged across an entire section.

Q3: Okay, yes, good idea.. here are some pics of the entertainment shelves. Does this work for understanding the browsing experience? The last pic is of a fiction case I think 🤔

A3: Yes—these are actually much more useful than individual close-ups for understanding the browsing experience. Looking at the shelves as a whole, a few patterns stand out.

1. Every shelf has "anchors."

Each section has a handful of books facing outward while the rest are displayed spine-out.

The face-out books naturally become visual entry points. Once a browser stops, they're much more likely to begin scanning the surrounding shelf.

2. The recommendation cards are surprisingly sparse.

One thing I noticed is that there aren't recommendation cards everywhere.

Instead, the store seems to be selective:

  • one recommendation every several feet,
  • one or two featured books,
  • then the shelves do the rest.

That restraint probably helps the recommendations feel special rather than overwhelming.

3. Different departments tell different stories.

The Young Adult shelves feel colorful and energetic, mixing bright contemporary fiction with fantasy.

The Music and Entertainment shelves feel much more exploratory. A reader might discover a memoir, then a history of rock, then a biography, then a book about songwriting—all within a few feet.

It's less about one bestseller and more about wandering.

4. The mystery shelves have a rhythm.

One fiction display follows a pattern that becomes obvious when viewed from a distance.

There are recognizable bestselling authors, then a featured recommendation, followed by familiar names again, another recommendation, and finally deeper catalog titles.

It balances confidence—"here's the book you've heard of"—with discovery—"here's something you might not have noticed."

5. A different kind of reader's guide.

One interesting observation from this visit is that bookstores and reader guides serve different purposes.

A bookstore helps people decide what to read.

A reader's guide helps people get more out of what they've already chosen to read.

Those aren't competing experiences. They're complementary ones.

6. The browsing experience itself.

One final suggestion emerged from the conversation:

Spend a few minutes simply watching how people browse. Notice whether they stop because of a cover, a recommendation card, an author's name, or whether they pick up several books before making a decision.

That observation adds another layer to understanding how readers discover books.

One final design observation also stood out.

Even though these shelves contain hundreds of books, they don't feel overwhelming. The face-out displays, generous spacing, and occasional recommendation cards create visual "rest stops" that make browsing feel manageable.

That's a design principle that translates surprisingly well to websites too—giving visitors clear places to pause instead of presenting everything all at once.

The simplest version of the discovery was this:

I was walking through a bookstore today and realized something.

The featured tables aren't there to tell you what to read.

They're more like points of interest.

Your own interests become the compass.

The bookstore simply gives you places to explore.
